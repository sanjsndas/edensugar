    The Quiet Art of Sourdough: A 36-Hour Journey

    By Elara Voss March 12, 2025 18 min read
    EV
    Elara Voss · Head baker at Eden Sugar House. Former pastry chef, now a devoted sourdough evangelist.

    There is a moment, somewhere around hour 30, when the dough finally feels alive. It's not just flour and water anymore — it's a living, breathing thing. This guide will take you through every step of a 36-hour sourdough process, from waking up your starter to pulling a blistered, crackling loaf from the oven.

    Why 36 hours?

    Time is the invisible ingredient. A long, cool fermentation develops flavor, improves digestibility, and creates that open, custardy crumb we chase. You can make decent bread in 6 hours. You can make remarkable bread in 36. The difference is night and day.

    Step 1: The starter (8–12 hours)

    Start with 50g of active starter, 100g of flour, and 100g of water. Mix and let it rise until doubled and bubbly. If your starter is sluggish, feed it twice a day for three days before baking.

    Step 2: Autolyse (1 hour)

    Mix 500g bread flour and 350g water. Rest for one hour. This hydrates the flour and begins gluten development without any kneading.

    Step 3: Mix and bulk ferment (4–6 hours)

    Add 100g starter, 10g salt. Perform four sets of stretch-and-folds spaced 30 minutes apart. Then let the dough rest until it's jiggly and about 50% larger.

    Step 4: Cold retard (12–18 hours)

    Shape the dough, place it in a banneton, cover, and put it in the fridge. This slow proof builds complex flavor and makes scoring easier.

    Step 5: Bake

    Preheat a Dutch oven to 500°F (260°C). Bake covered for 20 minutes, then uncovered at 450°F (230°C) for 25 minutes. Cool completely before slicing — if you can wait.

    Note: This is a living document. I'll keep adding notes as I test new flours and methods. — E.V.

    Keeping a Sourdough Starter: A Perpetual Guide

    By Marcus ReedMarch 10, 202514 min read
    MR
    Marcus Reed · Fermentation nerd and weekend baker.

    A sourdough starter is a pet that lives on your counter. But unlike a goldfish, it can survive neglect — if you know the signs. This guide covers feeding ratios, hydration, storage, and revival.

    Feeding 101

    Feed with equal weights of flour and water (100% hydration). For a standard jar, 50g starter + 50g flour + 50g water is plenty. If you bake weekly, keep it in the fridge and feed once a week.

    Signs of health

    Bubbles, domed top, yeasty smell (not acetone). If it smells like nail polish remover, it's hungry — feed it more often.

    Reviving a forgotten starter

    Pour off any dark liquid (hooch), scrape off the top layer, and feed with a 1:5:5 ratio. Repeat every 12 hours until it doubles reliably.

    Preserved Lemons: Salt, Time, and Transformation

    By Elara VossMarch 7, 202511 min read

    Preserved lemons are a staple of North African cooking, but they deserve a place in every pantry. The process couldn't be simpler: quarter lemons, pack with salt, add lemon juice, and wait. The result is a soft, intensely aromatic rind that adds a punch of umami to salads, tagines, and even pasta.

    Recipe: Basic preserved lemons

    • 6 organic lemons, scrubbed
    • 1/2 cup kosher salt
    • Additional lemon juice to cover

    Quarter lemons without cutting all the way through. Rub salt into the flesh. Pack tightly into a sterilized jar. Cover with lemon juice. Seal and let sit at room temperature for 3 days, then refrigerate for 3 weeks. Shake daily.

    How to use

    Rinse and discard the pulp, then mince the rind. Stir into yogurt, vinaigrettes, or grain bowls. It's magic.

    Frequently asked

    From our readers

    How do I know if my sourdough starter is ready?

    It should double in size within 4–6 hours of feeding, be full of bubbles, and smell pleasantly yeasty — not like acetone.

    Can I use honey instead of sugar in jam?

    Yes, but honey is sweeter and can overpower delicate fruit. Start with 3/4 cup honey per 4 cups of fruit and adjust.

    What's the best way to store homemade vanilla extract?

    In a cool, dark place, tightly sealed. It lasts indefinitely — just top up with alcohol as you use it.

    Do I need special equipment to start preserving?

    No. A large pot, clean jars, and a ladle are enough for most water-bath canning and refrigerator pickles.
